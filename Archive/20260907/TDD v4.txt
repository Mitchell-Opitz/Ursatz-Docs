# TECHNICAL DESIGN DOCUMENT V4

# General-Purpose Computational Music Platform

## Theory-Neutral Musical Representation, Semantics, Temporal Modeling, Interpretation, Analysis, Transformation, Generation, Performance, Notation, Corpus, and I/O

**Document Status:** Pre-Implementation Architecture
**Revision:** 4.0
**Supersedes:** V3
**Classification:** Foundational Architecture / Implementation Contract
**Primary Objective:** Define a theory-neutral computational foundation capable of representing, observing, interpreting, analyzing, transforming, generating, performing, rendering, importing, exporting, querying, and studying musical material without allowing any particular theory, notation system, storage technology, or generation technology to define the underlying musical domain.

---

# 1. Executive Summary

The platform shall be designed as a **general-purpose computational music system**, not as a software implementation of any particular theory of music.

The foundational architecture shall represent musical material independently of:

* music theory
* notation system
* instrument
* tuning system
* performance
* recording
* database
* serialization format
* rendering technology
* analytical methodology
* machine-learning architecture
* generation algorithm
* programming language

The central architectural principle is:

> **The system shall distinguish what musical material is represented from what is observed about it, what is inferred from it, how it is interpreted, under which framework it is interpreted, how it is transformed, and how it is ultimately rendered or performed.**

The architecture therefore shall contain distinct but interoperable domains:

```text
SOURCE / WORLD
    │
    ├── notation/document
    ├── performance
    └── recording
            │
            ▼
       OBSERVATIONS
            │
            ▼
   CANONICAL MUSICAL MODEL
            │
      ┌─────┼─────┐
      ▼     ▼     ▼
   events structures relationships
      │     │     │
      └─────┼─────┘
            ▼
        CONTEXT
            │
            ▼
      INTERPRETATIONS
            │
            ▼
      THEORY / KNOWLEDGE
            │
       ┌────┼────┐
       ▼    ▼    ▼
    ANALYSIS GENERATION TRANSFORMATION
       │    │    │
       └────┼────┘
            ▼
    CANONICAL MUSICAL MODEL
            │
      ┌─────┼──────────┐
      ▼     ▼          ▼
   NOTATION PERFORMANCE AUDIO
```

No layer above the canonical musical model may silently redefine the canonical model.

No interpretation may silently become authoritative musical fact.

No uncertainty may be discarded merely because a downstream subsystem prefers a single answer.

No generated object may lose its lineage.

No external representation may become the domain model merely because it is convenient to serialize.

---

# 2. Architectural Objective

The platform shall optimize for:

1. semantic correctness
2. theory neutrality
3. representational completeness
4. extensibility
5. provenance
6. uncertainty preservation
7. explainability
8. reproducibility
9. interoperability
10. testability
11. long-term maintainability
12. controlled performance optimization

The initial implementation shall prioritize semantic correctness over maximum throughput.

Optimization shall be evidence-driven.

The platform shall explicitly distinguish:

```text
Musical Material
Notation
Performance
Recording
Observation
Interpretation
Theory
Rule
Constraint
Preference
Heuristic
Statistical Model
Transformation

```

These concepts may refer to one another but shall not be collapsed into one universal object.

The architecture shall therefore avoid statements such as:

```text

Note = MIDI message
Note = performed sound
Note = scale degree

```

Instead:

```text

    ↓

    ↓

    ↓

    ↓
may be observed as
    ↓
may be interpreted as
```

# 4. Foundational Laws

The following laws are normative.

## Law 1 — Musical Material Is Not Theory

A theoretical framework may interpret musical material but shall not define the existence of the material.

## Law 2 — Representation Is Not Interpretation

A representation of an event does not imply what the event means.

## Law 3 — Notation Is Not Musical Reality

Notation is a representation/documentation of musical material.

## Law 4 — Performance Is Not Symbolic Content

Performance realization may differ from symbolic musical content.

## Law 5 — Recording Is Not Performance Semantics

A recording is captured signal/data. Musical meaning derived from it is an observation or interpretation.

## Law 6 — Relationships Are First-Class

Important relationships shall not be encoded solely as ad hoc fields.

## Law 7 — Context Matters

Claims whose validity depends on context shall explicitly identify that context.

## Law 8 — Interpretation Is Non-Authoritative by Default

Analytical claims shall not silently overwrite source musical material.

## Law 9 — Uncertainty Is Data

Unknown, ambiguous, competing, and probabilistic information shall remain representable.

## Law 10 — Provenance Is Preserved

Derived objects shall retain sufficient lineage to determine how they were produced.

## Law 11 — Theory Is Pluggable

The foundational kernel shall not depend on any particular music-theoretical framework.

## Law 12 — Rules and Statistics Are Different

A statistical tendency shall never automatically become a theoretical rule.

## Law 13 — Constraints and Preferences Are Different

A preference may rank candidates without invalidating them.

## Law 14 — External Formats Are Adapters

MIDI, MusicXML, ABC, MEI, audio formats, databases, JSON, protobuf, and similar systems shall not define the canonical domain.

## Law 15 — Storage Is Not Domain Semantics

A relational schema, graph database, document store, or event store shall remain replaceable.

## Law 16 — Identity Is Not Position

Array index, measure number, file offset, or database row number shall never be the sole identity of a persistent musical object.

## Law 17 — Occurrence Is Distinct from Conceptual Identity

A recurring motif and one occurrence of that motif are different concepts.

## Law 18 — Written and Sounding Pitch Are Distinct

Instrument transposition shall not be embedded into the universal definition of pitch.

## Law 19 — Symbolic Time Is Exact

Authoritative symbolic temporal values shall not depend on floating-point arithmetic.

## Law 20 — Transformations Do Not Mutate Their Inputs

Transformations shall normally create new versions or derived objects.

## Law 21 — Stochastic Processes Are Reproducible

Random generation shall support explicit seeds and reproducibility metadata.

## Law 22 — Loss Is Explicit

Conversions that cannot preserve information shall report the loss.

## Law 23 — Canonicalization Is Not Equality

Two objects may canonicalize to equivalent forms without being identical entities.

## Law 24 — Partial Information Is Valid Information

An incomplete object shall be representable when the domain permits incomplete knowledge.

## Law 25 — The Foundation Remains Small

New concepts shall be introduced above the kernel whenever possible.

# 6. World and Source Domain

A source is an external artifact or phenomenon from which information is obtained.

Examples:

```text
manuscript
printed score
digital score
recording
live performance
MIDI stream
audio file
human annotation
corpus
database
external analysis
```

A source shall have identity and provenance.

A source shall not be assumed to contain complete or authoritative information.

---

# 7. Digital Artifact

A DigitalArtifact represents an external digital object.

Examples:

```text
PDF
MusicXML document
MIDI file
MEI document
WAV file
JSON document
image
database export
```

Digital artifacts shall remain distinguishable from the musical material represented by them.

---

# 8. Canonical Musical Domain

The canonical musical domain represents musical material independently of serialization.

It shall contain concepts such as:

```text
Event
Occurrence
Container
Structure
Pitch
Duration
MusicalTime
Voice
Instrument
Relationship
```

The canonical domain shall not require JSON, XML, SQL, MIDI, or any other storage representation.

# 10. Entity

An Entity is an identifiable conceptual object.

Examples:

```text
Work
Instrument
Voice
Motif
Phrase
Framework
Rule
TransformationDefinition
```

A persistent entity shall normally have:

```text
id
type
schema_version
metadata
provenance_reference
```

---

# 11. Value Object

A Value represents immutable semantic content whose identity derives from its value.

Examples:

```text
Duration
MusicalTime
PitchClass
PitchSpelling
Interval
MeterSignature
Tempo
Ratio
Frequency
```

Value objects shall generally be immutable.

---

# 12. Occurrence

An Occurrence represents one situated instance of something.

Examples:

```text
MotifOccurrence
PhraseOccurrence
ThemeOccurrence
PerformanceOccurrence
NotationOccurrence
```

Occurrence is critical because conceptual identity and manifestation are different.

Example:

```text
Motif M1
    ├── occurrence O1
    ├── occurrence O2
    └── occurrence O3
```

A motif may exist as an abstract analytical object while occurrences identify regions in actual musical material.

---

# 13. Event

An Event represents an occurrence associated with a temporal domain.

Examples:

```text
NoteEvent
RestEvent
TempoEvent
MeterChangeEvent
ControlEvent
```

Events may be instantaneous or extended.

The temporal model shall not assume that every event is represented solely by:

```text
onset + duration
```

---

# 14. Event Identity

An event shall have stable identity.

Example:

```text
event-8e92...
```

Its identity shall not depend on:

```text
measure number
voice position
array index
MIDI tick
file location
```

---

# 15. Event Lifecycle

The system shall distinguish:

```text
creation
revision
derivation
supersession
deletion/tombstoning
```

where persistent storage requires historical traceability.

Deletion of an object from an application view shall not necessarily mean destruction of provenance.

---

# 16. Version Model

Identity and version are distinct.

Example:

```text
EntityID = M1
Version  = 4
```

A revision means:

```text
M1 version 4
```

A derived object means:

```text
M2
derived_from M1
```

These shall not be conflated.

---

# 17. Temporal Architecture

Time shall be explicitly multidimensional.

At minimum:

```text
MusicalTime
NotatedTime
AbsoluteTime
PerformanceTime
AudioTime
```

A system shall not assume these are interchangeable.

---

# 18. MusicalTime

MusicalTime represents symbolic temporal position.

The foundational representation shall support exact rational values.

Example:

```text
0
1/4
1/2
3/4
7/12
```

Conceptually:

```text
RationalTime {
    numerator: Integer
    denominator: PositiveInteger
}
```

The implementation may use a more optimized equivalent, but exact semantics shall be preserved.

---

# 19. Duration

Duration represents an amount of time.

Required invariant:

```text
duration >= 0
```

Duration shall support exact rational values.

Examples:

```text
1/4
1/8
3/16
5/12
```

---

# 20. Temporal Span

The system shall introduce an explicit temporal span abstraction.

```text
TimeSpan
    start
    end
    boundary semantics
```

This is preferable to assuming every object has `onset + duration`.

A span may be:

```text
closed
open
left_closed
right_closed
```

where the domain requires boundary distinctions.

---

# 21. Instant

Some events have no meaningful duration.

Examples:

```text
tempo change
meter change
key-signature declaration
control message
structural boundary
```

The system shall therefore support:

```text
TimeInstant
```

separately from:

```text
TimeSpan
```

---

# 22. Temporal Anchors

Objects may be anchored by:

```text
absolute musical position
relative position
container-relative position
event-relative position
notation position
performance timestamp
```

The anchor type shall be explicit.

---

# 23. Relative Time

The system shall support relationships such as:

```text
after Event A by 1/8
before Event B by 1/16
same onset as Event C
```

Relative temporal relationships shall not require conversion into absolute positions prematurely.

---

# 24. Meter

Meter describes metrical organization.

Example:

```text
4/4
3/4
6/8
5/8
```

Meter shall not itself determine all temporal semantics.

Meter shall be represented independently from duration.

---

# 25. MeterMap

A MeterMap associates metrical organization with temporal regions.

Conceptually:

```text
MeterMap
    region
    meter_signature
    effective_from
    optional beat structure
    optional hierarchy
```

Metrical interpretation shall be derivable from the map plus musical time.

---

# 26. Metrical Position

MetricalPosition may contain:

```text
measure_index
beat
subdivision
metrical_level
beat_strength
local_position
```

However, these are contextual coordinates rather than intrinsic properties of time.

---

# 27. Notated Position

Notated position shall be distinct from MusicalTime.

It may include:

```text
document
page
system
staff
measure
layer
symbol index
graphic coordinates
```

A notation document may encode multiple temporal interpretations.

---

# 28. Tempo

Tempo shall be modeled independently from symbolic musical time.

A tempo mapping may be:

```text
constant
piecewise constant
piecewise linear
continuous
performance-derived
algorithmically estimated
```

---

# 29. TempoMap

Conceptually:

```text
TempoMap:
    musical_time → performance_time
```

The mapping shall be capable of representing:

```text
tempo changes
accelerando
ritardando
rubato
tempo curves
performance deviations
```

---

# 30. PerformanceTime

PerformanceTime represents realized temporal coordinates.

It may contain:

```text
onset timestamp
release timestamp
inter-onset interval
timing deviation
local tempo
```

Performance timing shall not overwrite symbolic MusicalTime.

---

# 31. AudioTime

AudioTime shall represent positions in captured signal space.

Examples:

```text
sample index
frame index
seconds
```

AudioTime shall remain distinct from PerformanceTime because signal capture and interpreted performance timing are not identical.

---

# 32. Temporal Mapping

Mappings shall be first-class where necessary.

Examples:

```text
MusicalTime ↔ PerformanceTime
MusicalTime ↔ NotatedPosition
PerformanceTime ↔ AudioTime
```

Mappings may be exact, approximate, piecewise, or uncertain.

---

# 33. Pitch Architecture

Pitch shall be decomposed into independent dimensions.

```text
PitchIdentity
PitchClass
PitchSpelling
PitchRealization
TuningSystem
Frequency
PitchTrajectory
```

---

# 34. PitchIdentity

PitchIdentity represents abstract pitch information.

It shall not assume:

```text
12 pitch classes
equal temperament
A = 440 Hz
integer MIDI note numbers
```

---

# 35. PitchClass

PitchClass represents equivalence under a declared equivalence relation.

This is important:

> Pitch-class membership shall not be assumed to mean modulo-12 equivalence.

A pitch-class system shall therefore declare its equivalence model.

---

# 36. PitchSpelling

PitchSpelling represents notation-oriented pitch identity.

Example:

```text
letter = C
accidental = sharp
octave = 4
```

Spelling shall remain distinct from pitch realization.

Thus:

```text
C#
Db
```

may be realized at equivalent frequencies while retaining different spellings.

---

# 37. PitchRealization

PitchRealization represents a sounding or physically realizable pitch.

Possible representations:

```text
frequency
ratio
cents
fundamental
custom tuning coordinate
```

---

# 38. TuningSystem

Tuning shall be independent.

Examples:

```text
12-TET
24-TET
Pythagorean
Just Intonation
Meantone
custom ratio system
non-octave systems
microtonal systems
```

The system shall not hard-code A440.

---

# 39. Pitch Trajectory

Performance and audio analysis may require pitch to vary continuously.

The architecture shall therefore support:

```text
PitchTrajectory
    time
    pitch realization
    uncertainty
```

This permits:

```text
vibrato
glissando
pitch bend
portamento
microtonal drift
```

without corrupting symbolic pitch.

---

# 40. WrittenPitch and SoundingPitch

The system shall distinguish:

```text
WrittenPitch
SoundingPitch
```

An instrument transposition mapping may connect them.

---

# 41. Instrument

Instrument is a higher-level entity.

Possible properties:

```text
range
tessitura
transposition
clefs
articulations
techniques
dynamics
continuous controls
polyphony
physical constraints
preferred registers
```

These properties shall be configurable.

---

# 42. Instrument Capability

Instrument capability shall be modeled separately from instrument identity where practical.

Example:

```text
InstrumentCapability
    pitch_range
    simultaneous_note_limit
    articulation_support
    technique
    physical_constraints
```

This allows the same instrument to have multiple playing contexts.

---

# 43. Core Musical Events

The foundational event set shall remain small.

Initial candidates:

```text
NoteEvent
RestEvent
ControlEvent
```

Additional event types may be added where they represent genuine domain distinctions.

---

# 44. NoteEvent

A NoteEvent represents symbolic musical occurrence.

Minimum conceptual model:

```text
NoteEvent
    id
    time_anchor
    duration
    pitch
    voice_reference
    provenance_reference
```

Optional data shall be attached through separate structures when possible.

---

# 45. NoteEvent Is Not Necessarily a Sound

A NoteEvent may represent:

```text
notated note
symbolic note
analytical note event
generated note
```

Its sounding realization is a separate concern.

---

# 46. RestEvent

RestEvent represents an explicit symbolic absence in an appropriate context.

It shall not be interpreted as universal physical silence.

---

# 47. ControlEvent

ControlEvent may represent temporal changes not naturally modeled as notes.

Examples:

```text
pedal
control change
continuous control
program change
performance instruction
```

Protocol-specific concepts shall remain adapter-specific unless promoted into domain semantics.

---

# 48. Event Attachments

The architecture shall support attachable semantic objects for:

```text
articulation
dynamic
ornament
tie
slur
pedal
accent
expression
technique
notation
performance realization
```

This prevents NoteEvent from becoming a universal attribute bag.

---

# 49. Ties

A tie shall be represented as a relationship/semantic object.

A tie shall not require two note events to be merged.

This preserves:

```text
notation semantics
symbolic event identity
performance realization
analysis semantics
```

as separate concerns.

---

# 50. Slurs

Slurs shall be represented independently of ties.

A slur may indicate:

```text
phrasing
legato
performance instruction
analytical grouping
notation
```

depending on context.

---

# 51. Articulation

Articulation shall be modeled as an attachment or event-level semantic object.

Examples:

```text
staccato
tenuto
accent
marcato
fermata
```

The semantic and performance realization shall remain distinguishable.

---

# 52. Dynamics

Dynamics shall be modeled separately from pitch events.

Examples:

```text
p
mf
f
crescendo
diminuendo
```

Performance may realize these differently.

---

# 53. Voice

A Voice represents a logical stream or simultaneity domain.

A Voice shall not automatically imply:

```text
instrument
staff
part
harmony
melodic role
```

These relationships shall be explicit.

---

# 54. Part, Staff, Layer, Voice

The architecture shall distinguish:

```text
Part
Staff
Layer
Voice
```

because they are not universally equivalent.

A voice may appear on multiple staves.

A staff may contain multiple voices.

A part may contain multiple staves.

---

# 55. Containers

Containers group or organize objects.

Examples:

```text
Voice
Layer
Staff
Part
Measure
Phrase
Section
Movement
Work
```

Containment shall be explicit.

Containment shall not imply theoretical interpretation.

---

# 56. Structures

Structures organize musical material at higher semantic levels.

Examples:

```text
Motif
Figure
Phrase
Theme
Section
Movement
Work
```

Structures may reference events without duplicating them.

---

# 57. Structure Does Not Imply Hierarchy

The system shall permit arbitrary structural relationships.

A work need not have:

```text
Motif → Phrase → Theme → Section
```

as its universal hierarchy.

Hierarchical structure shall be optional.

---

# 58. Occurrences of Structures

A structure may be abstract while occurrences are situated.

Example:

```text
Motif M1
    occurrence O1 = events 1–4
    occurrence O2 = events 17–20
```

This enables pattern and form analysis without duplicating musical events.

---

# 59. Score Model

The score model is a specialized representation layer.

It may contain:

```text
Staff
Clef
Key Signature
Time Signature
Measure
Beam
Stem
Barline
Tuplet
Layout
Page
System
```

Score structures shall not redefine canonical musical events.

---

# 60. Notation Model

Notation shall be treated as a **document/representation model**, not merely a renderer.

This distinction is important.

A notation artifact may contain information that has no direct canonical-event equivalent:

```text
engraving decisions
page layout
graphic coordinates
beaming
stem directions
editorial markings
facsimile references
variant readings
source-document structure
```

The system shall preserve such information in the notation layer where required.

MEI is an important precedent: its scope includes not merely abstract notes but structured musical documents, metadata, notation, analysis, performances, and linking.

---

# 61. Performance Model

A Performance is a realization of musical material.

It may contain:

```text
performance events
actual onset
actual release
velocity
pitch realization
timing deviation
dynamic realization
articulation realization
pedal state
continuous controls
performer metadata
```

---

# 62. Performance Event

A PerformanceEvent shall reference its symbolic or conceptual source where known.

Example:

```text
PerformanceEvent P1
    realizes NoteEvent N1
```

But the relationship may be:

```text
one-to-one
one-to-many
many-to-one
unknown
```

This is essential for:

```text
ornamentation
arpeggiation
splitting
merging
repeated attacks
imperfect transcription
```

---

# 63. Recording

A Recording represents captured signal.

Example:

```text
Recording
    channels
    sample_rate
    duration
    encoding
    source
    metadata
```

A recording is not itself a musical interpretation.

---

# 64. Recording-to-Music Mapping

Audio analysis may produce:

```text
Observation
```

which may produce:

```text
Candidate
```

which may become:

```text
Interpretation
```

which may eventually produce:

```text
Canonical Musical Representation
```

This progression shall preserve uncertainty.

---

# 65. Semantic Layer

The semantic layer contains derived or framework-independent musical concepts.

Examples:

```text
Interval
Sonority
Contour
RhythmicPattern
PitchCollection
Scale
```

Some concepts such as `Chord` and `Key` require explicit contextual/framework semantics and therefore shall not automatically be treated as foundational facts.

---

# 66. Derived Semantics

A derived semantic object shall identify its source.

Example:

```text
Interval I1
    endpoint_a = N1
    endpoint_b = N2
    derivation = interval_calculation
```

Derived semantics may be materialized for:

```text
query
cache
analysis
indexing
```

without becoming source truth.

---

# 67. Interval

Interval shall support:

```text
directed interval
undirected interval
diatonic distance
chromatic distance
quality
compound interval
microtonal interval
ratio
cents
framework-specific interpretation
```

The interval representation shall identify which dimensions are actually known.

---

# 68. Sonority

Sonority represents a selected collection of simultaneously relevant pitch-bearing events.

Its selection semantics shall be explicit.

For example:

```text
time window
voice set
performance realization
symbolic events
sounding events
```

A Sonority is not automatically a Chord.

---

# 69. Chord

Chord shall be modeled as a semantic interpretation of a sonority or other material under a declared framework/context.

Example:

```text
Sonority S1
    ↓
Interpretation I1
    claim = C-major triad
    framework = tonal-harmony
```

---

# 70. Key

Key shall be contextual.

A note shall not intrinsically contain:

```text
scale degree
tonic function
Roman numeral
```

These shall be derived relative to context.

---

# 71. Scale

Scale shall represent an ordered pitch collection under declared semantics.

Possible attributes:

```text
root
ordered pitch collection
interval pattern
tuning
framework
orientation
```

---

# 72. Scale Degree

Scale degree is contextual.

Therefore:

```text
Pitch C
```

does not intrinsically equal:

```text
degree 1
```

A scale-degree assertion shall identify:

```text
target
context
framework
mapping
```

---

# 73. Contour

Contour represents relative change.

Examples:

```text
up
down
same
up-up-down
```

Contour may be computed independently from exact pitch.

---

# 74. Rhythmic Pattern

Rhythmic patterns shall be independently representable.

A rhythmic query shall not require pitch information.

---

# 75. Relationship Model

Relationships shall be first-class.

Conceptual model:

```text
Relationship
    id
    source
    predicate
    target
    scope
    context
    qualifiers
    evidence
    uncertainty
    provenance
```

---

# 76. Relationship Predicate

Predicates shall have metadata.

Example:

```text
PredicateDefinition
    name
    inverse
    symmetric
    transitive
    allowed_source_types
    allowed_target_types
    contextual
```

This prevents relationship semantics from being implicit.

---

Examples:

```text

overlaps

transformed_from
realizes
resembles
contrasts_with
interpreted_as
supports
contradicts
occurs_in
belongs_to
instantiates
has_occurrence
```

The vocabulary shall be extensible.

---

# 78. Relationship Scope

A relationship may have scope:

```text
global
work
movement
section
phrase
measure
voice
event
performance
analysis
```

---

# 79. Relationship Validity

Relationships may have:

```text
valid_from
valid_until
context
framework

```

---

# 80. Assertion

An Assertion is a claim made by an agent or process.

Examples:

```text
Note N belongs to phrase P
Phrase P is a cadence
Sonority S is a C-major chord
```

Assertions shall be distinct from the underlying objects they describe.

---

# 81. Observation

Observation records detected or measured information.

Examples:

```text
detected onset at 1.24 sec
OCR candidate C#
audio pitch estimate = 440 Hz
algorithm detected possible repetition
```

Observation does not imply interpretation.

---

# 82. Observation Structure

Conceptually:

```text
Observation
    id
    subject
    property
    value_or_candidates
    method
    source
    uncertainty
    timestamp
    provenance
```

---

# 83. Hypothesis

A Hypothesis represents a candidate explanation.

Examples:

```text
possible key = C major
possible chord = V/V
possible motif match
possible note spelling
```

Hypotheses shall support multiple candidates.

---

# 84. Uncertainty

Uncertainty shall be a first-class object.

It may represent:

```text
unknown
interval estimate
probability distribution
confidence score
candidate set
credible interval
qualitative uncertainty
competing hypotheses
```

A scalar confidence shall not be the universal uncertainty model.

---

# 85. Candidate Set

Example:

```text
CandidateSet
    C#  probability .73
    Db  probability .19
    C   probability .08
```

The architecture shall not require these probabilities to sum to one unless the uncertainty model explicitly says they represent a normalized distribution.

---

# 86. Unknown vs Low Confidence

The system shall distinguish:

```text
unknown
```

from:

```text
known approximately
```

and:

```text
known candidates with probabilities
```

These have different semantics.

---

# 87. Interpretation

Interpretation represents a contextual claim about musical material.

Conceptually:

```text
Interpretation
    target
    claim
    framework
    context
    evidence
    uncertainty
    author
    method
    status
    provenance
```

---

# 88. Interpretation Status

Possible states:

```text
proposed
supported
accepted
rejected
superseded
user_confirmed
machine_generated
imported
disputed
unknown
```

Status semantics shall be configurable.

---

# 89. Competing Interpretations

Multiple interpretations shall coexist.

Example:

```text
Interpretation A:
    C major

Interpretation B:
    E minor

Interpretation C:
    modal context
```

No global winner is required.

---

# 90. Context

Context defines conditions under which an assertion is evaluated.

Examples:

```text

metrical context
instrument context
performance context
historical context
analytical context
theoretical framework
local structural context
```

---

# 91. Context Composition

Contexts may be nested or composed.

Example:

```text

    ↓
Movement context
    ↓
Section context
    ↓
Phrase context
    ↓
Event context
```

A child context may inherit properties while overriding others.

The inheritance rules must be explicit.

---

# 92. Context Resolution

When an interpretation is evaluated, the system shall determine:

```text
applicable contexts
context precedence
inherited properties
overrides
conflicts
unknown values
```

---

# 93. Framework

A Framework defines an interpretive system.

Examples:

```text

Jazz Harmony

Neo-Riemannian Theory

```

Frameworks shall consume canonical domain interfaces.

They shall not modify foundational objects merely to implement theory-specific semantics.

---

# 94. Framework Versioning

Every nontrivial framework shall be versioned.

Example:

```text
framework_id = common_practice_harmony
framework_version = 2.1
```

Rule evaluations shall record the framework version used.

---

# 95. Rule

A Rule is a formal evaluative statement.

Example:

```text

```

A rule shall define:

```text

conditions
evaluation
severity

```

---

# 96. Constraint

A Constraint restricts candidate states.

Types:

```text
hard
soft
weighted
conditional
contextual
```

Constraints shall be machine-evaluable where used for generation.

---

# 97. Preference

A Preference ranks candidates without necessarily invalidating them.

Example:

```text
prefer stepwise motion
```

---

# 98. Heuristic

A heuristic is a strategy or estimate.

Example:

```text
favor likely cadential continuation
```

Heuristics shall not be represented as universal truths.

---

# 99. Tendency

A Tendency represents observed statistical behavior.

Example:

```text
transition X → Y occurs frequently in corpus C
```

A tendency shall never automatically imply:

```text
must
must not
valid
invalid
```

---

# 100. Statistical Model

Statistical models shall be explicit objects.

Example:

```text
StatisticalModel
    input_features
    output
    probability
    training_corpus
    model_version
    calibration
```

A model's output shall not become a theory rule automatically.

---

# 101. Procedure

A Procedure describes an algorithmic method.

Examples:

```text
Roman numeral analysis
cadence detection
phrase segmentation
motif search
species generator
```

A procedure shall declare:

```text
inputs
outputs
configuration
version
dependencies
determinism
```

---

# 102. Rule Evaluation

Conceptually:


Results:

```text

```

---

# 103. Evidence

Evidence is a structured basis for an assertion.

Examples:

```text

interval relationship
voice-leading pattern
corpus statistic
user annotation
external scholarly source
rule result
machine prediction
```

Evidence shall be distinguishable from the interpretation it supports.

---

# 104. Evidence Strength

Evidence may have:

```text

reliability
independence
method

```

A single scalar evidence score shall not be required.

# 105. Provenance

Provenance shall be modeled explicitly rather than merely as a text field.

The system should conceptually support the provenance pattern:

```text

```

This aligns naturally with established provenance modeling such as W3C PROV, whose data model explicitly represents entities, activities, agents, derivations, collections and related provenance information.

The platform need not implement W3C PROV literally, but should remain mappable to it.

# 106. Provenance Activity

Examples:


# 107. Provenance Agent

An Agent may be:

```text

```

A derivation records:

```text

```

# 109. Event-Level Transformation Provenance

Transformations shall preserve mappings between source and output events where meaningful.

Example:

```text
Output Note N7
    derived_from:
        Input Note N2
    transformation:
        transposition +P5
```

For one-to-many transformations:

```text
Input N2
    → N7
    → N8
    → N9
```

The mapping shall be retained.

# 110. Provenance Graph

Provenance should normally be acyclic.

Example:

```text

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

```

Cycles shall be prohibited unless explicitly supported by a future provenance model.

---

# 111. Audit History vs Provenance

The architecture shall distinguish:

```text
provenance
```

from:

```text
audit history
```

Provenance answers:

> Where did this artifact come from?

Audit history answers:

> Who changed what, when, and why?

Both may be required.

---

# 112. Authored Content

Human-authored analysis shall remain identifiable.

Example:

```text
Annotation
    author

    claim
    context
    framework
    timestamp
```

A user accepting a machine interpretation shall not erase the machine origin.

---

# 113. Promotion

A derived result may be promoted to authored content.

Example:

```text
Machine:
    possible V/V

User:
    accepts
```

The promoted assertion shall retain:

```text
machine hypothesis

timestamp
original evidence
original provenance
```

---

# 114. Equality Semantics

The system shall explicitly distinguish:

```text
identity equality
value equality
structural equality
semantic equality
canonical equality
```

---

# 115. Identity Equality

Two objects are identity-equal if they refer to the same entity/version under the domain's identity rules.

---

# 116. Value Equality

Two value objects are equal if their semantic values are equal.

---

# 117. Structural Equality

Two objects are structurally equal if their corresponding structure is equivalent under declared structural rules.

---

# 118. Semantic Equality

Two objects may be semantically equivalent despite differing representations.

Example:

```text
different spellings
```

may be semantically equivalent under a particular tuning/context.

---

# 119. Canonical Equality

Canonical equality means that two representations normalize to the same canonical form under a specified normalization policy.

Normalization policy must be versioned.

---

# 120. Canonicalization

Canonicalization shall not destroy meaningful distinctions.

For example:

```text
C#
Db
```

must not be normalized to one spelling if the distinction is meaningful to notation or theory.

---

# 121. Partial Information

Objects shall support partial knowledge where necessary.

Examples:

```text
pitch = unknown
pitch_candidates = [...]
duration = known
spelling = unknown
```

The system shall not force placeholders to masquerade as facts.

---

# 122. Musical IR

The canonical IR shall represent musical content using domain semantics rather than transport syntax.

A minimal conceptual note:

```text
NoteEvent {
    id
    temporal_anchor
    duration
    pitch_identity
    optional_pitch_spelling
    optional_voice
    provenance
}
```

The actual serialization is separate.

---

# 123. Canonical IR Requirements

The canonical IR shall support:

```text
exact time

multiple tuning systems
multiple pitch representations
stable identity
partial information
relationships
contexts
provenance
versioning
extensions
```

---

# 124. Serialization

Serialization converts canonical objects to external syntax.

Examples:

```text
JSON
protobuf
CBOR
XML
binary
```

No serialization format shall define the domain semantics.

---

# 125. Canonical Serialization

A canonical serialization should provide:

```text

explicit null/unknown semantics
extension mechanism
canonical ordering
migration support
```

---

Serialization:



Operations that create related objects shall have explicit consistency semantics.

# 175. MIDI Boundary

MIDI shall not define the core model.

MIDI 1.0's channels, ticks and message structure are transport/encoding concepts rather than universal musical semantics.

The architecture must also avoid assuming that MIDI 1.0 is the ceiling of performance representation. Current MIDI 2.0 introduces UMP and higher-resolution/per-note expressive capabilities, reinforcing the need for a richer performance boundary.

---

# 176. MIDI Mapping

The MIDI adapter may map:

```text
NoteEvent
 →
MIDI note messages
```

but may lose:

```text
spelling
analytical meaning
exact notation
non-MIDI tuning information
structural semantics
```

Loss must be reported.

---

# 177. MusicXML / MEI Boundary

MusicXML and MEI shall remain external representations.

However, notation-specific information that cannot be reduced safely to canonical events shall remain available through a notation/document layer.

The architecture shall not make the mistake of treating notation as disposable merely because abstract musical events are canonical.

MEI's current model is a useful reminder that machine-readable music documents can encompass structural, notational, analytical, performance and linking information beyond a simple event sequence.

---

# 178. Loss Model

```text

unknown
```

```text

    velocity = preserved as performance data
    spelling = unknown
    engraving = unavailable
```

# 261. Ultimate Architectural Objective

The platform is not:

> software that knows music theory.

It is:

> **a general-purpose computational language and platform for representing musical material, temporal organization, pitch, events, structures, relationships, observations, interpretations, context, evidence, uncertainty, provenance, theoretical frameworks, transformations, constraints, performances, notation, recordings, and generation processes.**

The system must be able to represent:

```text
what exists
what occurred
where it occurred
when it occurred
how it is organized
what relates to what
what was observed
what is hypothesized
what is interpreted
under which framework
with what evidence
with what uncertainty
by whom or by what process
from which source
through which transformation
under which constraints
according to which model
and with what resulting realization
```

while keeping all of those dimensions computationally distinguishable.

The final governing principle is therefore:

> **Freeze the semantics of the foundation, not the ontology of every future musical idea. Keep the kernel small; make relationships, context, interpretation, uncertainty, provenance, and extension mechanisms first-class; preserve distinctions between symbolic material, notation, performance, and recording; and ensure that no theory, storage system, notation format, statistical model, or generation technology can become the definition of music.**
